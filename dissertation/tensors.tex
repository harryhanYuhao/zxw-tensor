\section{Tensors and Basic Operations}

We present the following straightforward definition of tensors, which is a rephrasal of Penrose's original definition \cite{penrose1971applications}.

\begin{definition}\label{def:tensor}
Given $n\in \N$, let $[n]$ denote the set $\{0, 1, \cdots, n-1\}$. 
A tensor of rank $k \in \N$ is a function $T : [n_0] \times [n_1] \times \cdots [n_{k-1}] \rightarrow \C$.
\end{definition}

By covention, we shall write the value $T(i_0, i_1, \cdots, i_{k-1})$ as $T_{i_0 i_1 \cdots i_{k-1}}$, where each $i$ is called an index.
Of course, the output of $T$ can be $\R$ or any other ring. 
We ordain it to be $\C$ in this dissertation.
$n_j$ is called the dimension of the $j$-th index, which will always be clear from the context.

Following the convention of Einstein summation, a tensor $T$ is denoted by a capital letter with its indices as free variables shown in the subscript.
The class of all tensors is denoted by the caligraphy letter $\T_{ijk \cdots}$.

Tensor is, plainly and unonstensibly, an arrangement of complex numbers with some orderings by the indices.
For example, a rank-one tensor is a vector entries, rank-two tensor is a matrix, and a rank-three tensor is an arrangement of matrices.

The following equation denotes a vector and a matrix, while figure \ref{fig:rank3-stack} depicts the rank-three tensor as a stack of $n$ such matrix slices.
\begin{equation*}
	T_i =
	\begin{pmatrix}
		T_0 \\ T_1 \\ \vdots \\ T_{n-1}
	\end{pmatrix},
	\qquad
	T_{ij} =
	\begin{pmatrix}
		T_{00} & T_{01} & \cdots & T_{0,n-1} \\
		T_{10} & T_{11} & \cdots & T_{1,n-1} \\
		\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
		T_{n-1,0} & T_{n-1,1} & \cdots & T_{n-1,n-1}
	\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}

There are at least seven natural operations for tensors.
\begin{observation}
	\begin{itemize} 
		\item \textbf{Addition} $\T + \T \rightarrow \T$. Let $T_{ijk \cdots}$ and $S_{ijk \cdots}$ be two tensors of the same rank. Then their sum is defined by $(T + S)_{ijk \cdots} = T_{ijk \cdots} + S_{ijk \cdots}$.
		\item \textbf{Scalar multiplication} Let $T_{ijk \cdots}$ be a tensor and $c \in \C$. Define $(cT)_{ijk \cdots} =c (T_{ijk \cdots}) $
		\item \textbf{Permutation.} Let $T_{ijk\cdots}$ be a tensor of rank $m$. For any permutation $\sigma \in S_m$, the permuted tensor is defined by $(\sigma \cdot T)_{i_{\sigma(1)} i_{\sigma(2)} \cdots i_{\sigma(k)}} = T_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_k}$.
		\item \textbf{Flatten} $\T \rightarrow \T$. Let $T_{ijk \cdots}$ be a tensor. 
		Any of the two indices of $T$ can be flattened into one to create a new tensor.
		Say we would like to flatten the indices $i$ and $j$, of dimension $n_1, n_2$. Let $\alpha$ be an index of dimension $n_1n_2$, and we define the new tensor $S_{ak \cdots}$ as $S_{(n_2 i + j)k \cdots} = T_{ijk\cdots}$.
		\item \textbf{Unflatten}, which is the operation exactly opposite of flatten.
		\item \textbf{Outer product} $\T \otimes \T \rightarrow \T$ Let $T_{ijk \cdots}$ and $S_{lmn \cdots}$ be any two tensors.
		Their tensor product is defined by $(T \otimes S)_{ijklmn\cdots} = T_{ijk\cdots} S_{lmn\cdots}$. Outer product is sometimes called \textbf{tensor product} or \textbf{Kronecker product}.
		\item \textbf{Contraction.} Let $T_{ijk\cdots}$ and $S_{ijk \cdots}$ be tensor of rank $m$ and $n$. Their contraction over the index $i$ is a tensor of rank $m+n - 2$ defined by $(T \cdot S)_{jk\cdots lm\cdots} = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} T_{ijk\cdots} S_{ilm\cdots}$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{observation}

Addition and scalar multiplication of tensors are clear enough. 
Permutation of indices is generalisation of matrix transpose. 
For an example, consider ranked-two tensor $T$, and define $S_{ij} = T_{ji}$

\begin{equation*}
	T_{ij} =
	\begin{pmatrix}
		T_{00} & T_{01} & \cdots  \\
		T_{10} & T_{11} & \cdots \\ 
		\vdots & \vdots & \ddots \\
	\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
	S_{ij} =
	\begin{pmatrix}
		T_{00} & T_{10} & \cdots  \\
		T_{01} & T_{11} & \cdots \\ 
		\vdots & \vdots & \ddots \\
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation*}

Usually, outer product and flattenning are used together to convert tensor products of two arbitrary tensors into a single matrix, for example
\begin{equation}
	A \otimes \vec{v} = 
	\begin{pmatrix}
		a_{00} & a_{01}\\
		a_{10} & a_{11}
	\end{pmatrix}
	\begin{pmatrix}
		v_0 \\ v_1	
	\end{pmatrix}
	= 
	\begin{pmatrix}
		a_{00} v_0 & a_{01} v_0 \\
		a_{10} v_0 & a_{11} v_0 \\
		a_{00} v_1 & a_{01} v_1 \\
		a_{10} v_1 & a_{11} v_1
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

Contraction is the more complicated operation. 
For the matrices, $M_{ij}$ and $N(jk)$, their contraction over the index $j$ is a matrix $P_{ik}$ defined by $P_{ik} = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} M_{ij} N_{jk}$, which is exactly the matrix multiplication.
Other contraction operations are generalisation of matrix multiplication, which we will discuss in the next section.

\subsection{A Note on Abstract Tensor Product}

In multilinear algebra, the definition of tensor product is thus. 

\begin{definition}[Tensor Product of Vector Spaces]
	Let $V, W$ be vector spaces over a field $\F$. Their tensor product, denoted as $V \otimes W$, is the unique vector space over $\F$ that satisfies the following universal property as demonstrated in figure \ref{fig:tensor-product-universal}:
	there exists a bilinear map $\eta: V\times W \rightarrow V \otimes W $, and for all bilinear map $f : V \times W \rightarrow U$ to any vector space $U$, there exists a unique linear map $\hat{f} : V \otimes W \rightarrow U$ such that $f = \hat{f} \circ \eta$. 
\end{definition}

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\centering \input{./figures/tensor-product-universal.tikz}
	\caption{Commutative triangle for tensor product.}\label{fig:tensor-product-universal}
\end{figure}

Equivalently, the tensor product of two finite-dimensional vector spaces $V$ and $W$, with basis $v_1, \cdots, v_n$ and $w_1, \cdots, w_m$ is the $n\cdot m$ dimensional vector space $V \otimes W$ with basis $v_i \otimes w_j$ that satifies the following bilinearity conditions:
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{aligned}
		(v_i + v_j) \otimes w_k &= v_i \otimes w_k + v_j \otimes w_k, \\
		v_i \otimes (w_j + w_k) &= v_i \otimes w_j + v_i \otimes w_k, \\
		c (v_i \otimes w_j) &= (c v_i) \otimes w_j = v_i \otimes (c w_j), \quad c \in \F.
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
where $i, j, k$ are the appropriate indices of the basis vectors.

I hope the original definitions of tensor product justifies our nomenclature of tensor in definition \ref{def:tensor}.
It is clear that 
\begin{enumerate}
	\item A rank-one tensor, $T_i$, is a vector in some vector space $V$,
		where the dimension of the index $i$ is the dimension of $V$.
	\item In general, a rank-$k$ tensor $T_{i_1 i_2 \cdots}$ have additions and scalar multiplications, so the set of all rank-$k$ tensor with fixed dimension for each of its index, denoted by $\T_{i_1, i_2 \cdots}$, is a vector space.
	This vector space has a set of basis $\{e_{j_1, j_2, \cdots, j_k}, \cdots\}$ , where $e_{j_1, j_2, \cdots,}(i_1, i_2, \cdots) = 1$ if and only if $i_1 = j_1, i_2 = j_2, \cdots$, and $0$ otherwise.
	It is clear that this basis is in one-to-one correspondence with the tensor product of vector spaces $V_0, V_1, \cdots, V_k$, where dimension of $V_i$ is the dimension of the index $i$.
	\item Flatten is the canonical isomorphism between the vector space $V_1 \otimes V_2 \otimes \cdots \otimes V_k \rightarrow (V_1 \otimes V_2) \otimes \cdots \otimes V_k$. (And unflatten its inverser.)
	\item Outer product $(\T_1, \T_2) \rightarrow \T_1 \otimes \T_2$ is a bilinear form, therefore this map is the canonical bilinear map guaranteed by the universal property of tensor product.
\end{enumerate}

In linear algebra, there is an intrinsic notion of input and output in linear algebra. 
For example, in vector space $V$ a vector is an element of $V$. Its dual is a function $V \rightarrow \C$, and a matrix is a function $V \rightarrow V$.
In physics, the input and output of tensor are differentiated by the upper and lower indices. 
For example, $\T^k_l$ denotes the class of tensor $ \underbrace{V \otimes V \otimes \cdots \otimes V}_{k} \otimes \underbrace{V^* \otimes V^* \otimes \cdots \otimes V^*}_{l} \cong \underbrace{V \otimes  \cdot \otimes V}_{l} \rightarrow \underbrace{V \otimes \cdots \otimes V}_{k}$,
where $V$ is a vector space and $V^*$ is its dual.
While differentiating input and ouputs seems an intuitive idea, it actually is unweldy in practice.
As our investigation of tensor product in this thesis is of an algorithmic nature, we will not differentiate input from output.

\section{Tensor Networks}

Suppose we have two tensors $T_{ijk}$ and $S_{kl}$, where index $i $ and $j$ have dimension $n_1$ and $n_2$ while both of index $k$ and $l$ have dimension $n_1$.
Suppose we would like to perform the following operation: `contracting the first index of $T$ with the second index of $S$, and contracting the last index of the resulting tensor with the first index another copy of $T$.' 
The precise statement of specifying operations of tensor is too verbose and painful to articulate.

Tensor network, a graphical representation of tensors and their operations, is used to make these statement concise and clear.

\textbf{Rule 1}. In tensor networkA rank-$k$ tensor is represented as a node with $k$ edges.

\section{Complexity of Contraction}

Let us investigate the algorithmic complexity of tensor conctraction, which is a a problem of paramount importance as it is used in so many applications, such as quantum circuit simulation, machine learning, and almost all kinds of mathematics modelling.

For simplicity, we assume all index in this section have dimension $d$.

Contraction of two rank-2 tensors, $M_{ij}$ and $N_{jk}$, gives another rank-2 tensor $P_{ik}$.
Each entry of $P$ is computed using the formula
$$
	P_{ik} = \sum_{j=0}^{d-1} M_{ij} N_{jk}	
$$
Computing a single entry thus takes $d$ multiplications and $d-1$ additions.
There are a total of $d^2$ entries to compute, so the total number of operations is $d^2(d + d - 1) = O(d^3)$.

Similarly, contraction of on bond between a rank $m$ and a rank $n$ tensor will result in a rank $m + n - 2$ tensor. 
Each entry of ther resulting tensor is computed by summing over $d$ products of entries from the two tensors, so the total number of operations is $O(d^{m+n-1})$.

We may also choose to contract multiple bonds simultaneously. 
In general, contracting $k$ bonds between a rank $m$ and a rank $n$ tensor will result in a rank $m + n - 2k$ tensor.
Each entry of the resulting tensor is computed by summing over $d^k$ products of entries from the two tensors, so the total number of operations is $O(d^{m+n-k})$.

It is thus clear that contracting $k$ bonds simultaneously over a tensor network with $n$ external bonds will take $O(d^{n+k})$ operations.
This is an important observation that worth to be written down.

\begin{lemma}[Complexities of Tensor Contraction by Counting Edges]\label{lem:contraction-complexity}
	Let $T$ be a tensor network with $n$ external bonds and $k$ internal bonds. 
	Contracting all the internal bonds of $T$ will take $O(d^{n+k})$ operations.
\end{lemma}

It may surprise the reader that different order of contraction will lead to different complexities. 
For example, contracting the following tensor fully, which will gives a scalar, can be done in two different orders.
