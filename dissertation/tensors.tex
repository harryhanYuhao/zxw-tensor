\chapter{Tensors} \label{ch:tensors}

\section{Tensors, Contraction, and Tensor Network}

We present the following straightforward definition of tensors, which is a rephrasal of Penrose's original definition \cite{penrose1971applications}.

\begin{definition}
Let $[n]$ denote the set $\{0, 1, \cdots, n-1\}$. 
A tensor of rank $k \in \N$ is a function $T : [n]^k \rightarrow \C$.
\end{definition}

By covention, we shall write $T(i_1, i_2, \cdots, i_k)$ as $T_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_k}$, where each $i$, which is called an index, shall be an integer from 0 to $n-1$ inclusively. 
Of course, $T$ can be a function that maps $[n]^k$ to $\R$ or any other ring. We ordain it to be $\C$ in this dissertation.

Tensor is, plainly and unonstensibly, an arrangement of $n^k$ complex numbers with some orderings by the indices.
For example, a rank-one tensor is a vector of $n$ entries, rank-two tensor is an $n$ times $n$ matrix, and a rank-three tensor is an arrangement of $n$ matrices.

The following equation denotes a vector and a matrix, while figure \ref{fig:rank3-stack} depicts the rank-three tensor geometrically as a stack of $n$ such matrix slices.
\begin{equation*}
	T_i =
	\begin{pmatrix}
		T_0 \\ T_1 \\ \vdots \\ T_{n-1}
	\end{pmatrix},
	\qquad
	T_{ij} =
	\begin{pmatrix}
		T_{00} & T_{01} & \cdots & T_{0,n-1} \\
		T_{10} & T_{11} & \cdots & T_{1,n-1} \\
		\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
		T_{n-1,0} & T_{n-1,1} & \cdots & T_{n-1,n-1}
	\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
